Day 3 · In-person

Water cycle in a bag

Students build a mini, sealed water cycle they can watch run in real time on a sunny window — evaporation, condensation, and "rain" all inside a sandwich bag.

ModeIn-person
FocusHands-on lab
Prep time10 min
Class time40 min

Learning objective

Students will observe evaporation and condensation happening together in a closed system, and connect what they see in the bag to the vocabulary they'll use for the rest of the unit: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, collection.

Materials

Per student (or per pair)

  • 1 quart-size clear ziplock bag
  • Blue food coloring (a few drops, optional — makes the "ocean" water easy to spot)
  • Water, about 1/4 cup
  • Permanent marker
  • Painter's tape or masking tape
  • A sunny window

Lesson timing

  • 0–5 min
    Hook. Revisit the "mystery puddle" question from Day 1: if water disappears from a puddle, where does it actually go? Take a few guesses without confirming or correcting yet.
  • 5–15 min
    Build. Students draw a simple sun, cloud, and wavy "ocean" line on the outside of their bag in marker, add water (tinted blue if using food coloring), seal the bag almost all the way, press out excess air, then seal completely.
  • 15–20 min
    Tape up. Tape each bag to a sunny window at a slight downward angle, water pooled at the bottom. Label each with initials.
  • 20–30 min
    Predict + record. In science notebooks, students sketch the bag as it looks right now and write one prediction: what do they expect to see happen over the next hour, and by tomorrow?
  • 30–40 min
    Discuss. Whole-class discussion connecting the setup to the real water cycle: the water at the bottom is the ocean/lake, the sun's heat drives evaporation, water droplets forming on the inside of the bag are condensation, and droplets running back down are precipitation and collection.

Step-by-step procedure

  1. Draw the scene. On the outside of the bag, students draw a sun in one corner and a wavy line about a third of the way up to represent the ocean.
  2. Add water. Pour about 1/4 cup of water into the bag — tinted blue if using food coloring — so it sits below the drawn "ocean" line.
  3. Seal and mount. Press most of the air out, seal the bag, and tape it to a sunny window with the water pooled at the bottom corner.
  4. Observe over time. Check back after about an hour, then again the next morning. Water droplets should appear on the upper part of the bag (condensation) and begin trickling back down (precipitation/collection).
  5. Label the cycle. Once droplets are visible, students label their bag sketch with the four vocabulary terms at the point in the cycle where each occurs.

Watch for these misconceptions

Common mix-ups to address during discussion

  • Students sometimes think the water "disappears" rather than changing state — reinforce that it's still there, just as invisible water vapor.
  • Some will call any droplet on the bag "rain," even before it's condensed and started falling — precipitation is specifically water falling or sliding down, not just condensed droplets sitting in place.
  • Students may think the water cycle needs the sun's heat only for evaporation — point out that cooling near the top of the bag (cooler than the sunny bottom) is what causes condensation.
